Definition

What is toxinology?

The study of toxins produced by living organisms: their biology, chemistry, effects and treatment.

Toxinology

Natural toxins and venoms from animals, plants, bacteria and fungi. Encompasses biology, chemistry, pharmacology and clinical medicine.

Toxicology

The broader science of poisons, including synthetic chemicals, drugs and industrial agents (toxicants).

Clinical relevance

Why toxinology belongs in the ED

01

Time-sensitive

Paralysis, coagulopathy and shock can evolve over minutes to hours. Serial assessment beats a single snapshot.

02

ABC can fail

Neurotoxins → respiratory failure. Haematotoxins → bleeding and shock. Myotoxins → hyperkalaemia and AKI.

03

Specific antidote exists

Antivenom is definitive therapy, but it is scarce, imported and can itself cause anaphylaxis.

04

A differential to keep in mind

Unexplained ptosis, bleeding or dark urine in a rural patient? Ask about bites and stings.

Common toxinological emergencies

  • Snakebite: pit vipers, cobras, kraits, sea snakes
  • Scorpion and centipede stings
  • Marine envenomation: jellyfish, stonefish, cone snails
  • Bee and wasp stings: anaphylaxis, mass envenomation
  • Plant and mushroom poisoning

In Malaysia, snakebite is the toxinological emergency you are most likely to manage. The rest of this lecture is about it.

Morphology clues

Elapid vs pit viper

Elapidae cobra · krait · coral · sea snake

  • Long, thin body, smooth scales; no loreal scale
  • N. sumatrana can spit venom ≥1 m at the eyes
  • Sea snakes: paddle-like flattened tail

Viperidae pit vipers

  • Stout body, broad triangular head
  • Many are arboreal (green pit vipers)
  • Malayan pit viper: ground dweller in plantations, northern states

Caution: there is no simple rule for spotting a venomous snake, and harmless mimics exist. Use a photo of the snake if one is available and get an expert to verify (RECS). Don't use random internet pictures, and never try to capture the snake.

In hospital · MOH 2017 §6.1–6.3

Triage, then ABCDE

Red zone

Any possible systemic or local envenomation.

Yellow zone

Asymptomatic patients, under close observation.

Monitor every 5–15 min

BP · HR · RR · SpO₂ · pain score · PEFR · hourly urine output (note colour in sea snake bites)

A

Airway

Head tilt–chin lift and suction; position to protect the airway. OPA only if there is no gag reflex.

B

Breathing

Neurotoxic paralysis → respiratory failure. Give O₂, use the BVM, then a definitive airway when indicated.

C

Circulation

Shock from venom, hypovolaemia or haemorrhage. Two large-bore IV lines, fluids, control bleeding, cardiac monitor. CPR if arrested.

D

Disability

Conscious level, ptosis, muscle weakness: a baseline for serial comparison.

E

Exposure

Find the bite, other bites, bleeding sites. Immobilise the limb in a neutral position at heart level.

How to measure RPP: stick a time-labelled tape at the leading edge each hour. RPP = distance between the two most recent labels ÷ hours between them.

Guideline example: 2.5 cm in 4 h = 0.6 cm/h. Keep only the two most recent labels.

Measuring limb circumference is not recommended. Venom swelling spreads proximally and is not compartment syndrome. Ultrasound shows a “cobblestone” pattern of subcutaneous oedema.

Serial bloods during the first 48 h after the bite. A bite by a non-venomous snake, confirmed by an expert, with no signs of envenoming may need no bloods.

Bedside test · MOH 2017 §4.5.9

20WBCT: try it

Patient:
1Place 2 ml of fresh venous blood in a new or heat-cleaned, dry glass tube.
2Leave it undisturbed for 20 minutes at room temperature.
3Tip the tube once. Still liquid = blood unclotted = coagulopathy likely.

Use it for an unidentified bite, a suspected pit viper bite, or when lab coagulation tests are unavailable. Repeat as needed. Never the sole basis for giving antivenom: interpret it clinically and confirm with the coagulation profile.

Admit & observe

  • Local and/or systemic envenomation
  • Bite by a venomous snake
  • Unidentified snakebite
  • Unidentified animal bite

Safe to discharge only if…

  • A trained expert positively identifies the snake as non-venomous
  • The clinical picture matches that species
  • No significant tissue injury or persistent bleeding

Exceptions: Boiga can cause tissue injury; Rhabdophis subminiatus can cause coagulopathy.

MOH 2017 §6.4–6.5

General care, and the don'ts

  • Immobilise the limb in a neutral position; chart neurovascular status
  • Anti-tetanus as indicated
  • Analgesia: paracetamol for mild pain; titrated IV opioid for moderate to severe pain
  • Antibiotics only for necrosis or extensive tissue injury (e.g. python); cover aerobes and anaerobes
  • Eye exposure: irrigate with 5–10 L of saline
  • Life-threatening bleeding: blood products while waiting for AV, but they are not a substitute for it
  • Neurotoxic: consider a trial of neostigmine + atropine (cobra, not krait)
Definitive: Antivenom Give as soon as it is indicated

Don't!

✗No NSAIDs or aspirin, especially when coagulopathy is possible
✗No IM injections after a pit viper bite (haematoma risk); use oral or IV
✗No prophylactic hydrocortisone or antihistamine unless there is allergy or anaphylaxis; sedating antihistamines also cloud the neuro assessment
✗No tranexamic acid or vitamin K: they don't work in venom coagulopathy
✗No arterial puncture if coagulopathy is suspected
✗No fasciotomy on the basis of swelling alone
Immunotherapy · MOH 2017 §7.1

What is antivenom?

Immunoglobulins (or fragments of them) from animals immunised with venom. They bind toxins, inactivate them and speed their elimination.

Whole IgG~150 kDa
F(ab')₂~100 kDa
Fab~50 kDa

Monovalent

Raised against one species, e.g. QSMI Malayan pit viper AV. Best when the snake is identified.

Polyvalent

Raised against several species and used by syndrome: NPAV (neuro) and HPAV (haemato).

Malaysia imports its antivenom

Mostly from the Queen Saovabha Memorial Institute (QSMI), Thai Red Cross, because Thai snakes are closely related to ours.

Elapid antivenoms have lower potency, so the starting dose is higher (up to 10 vials). Indian polyvalent AV has weak or no effect against most Malaysian venoms and is not recommended.

Antivenom can reverse systemic envenoming even weeks later, but it prevents local necrosis only if given within a few hours.

MOH 2017 §7.3.1 · all antivenom is given IV

How to give antivenom

Adrenaline first

Draw it up before starting: 0.5 mg IM for adults, 0.01 mg/kg for children (1:1000). No premedication.

1

Reconstitute

With the supplied diluent or 10 ml WFI. Swirl gently; never shake.

2

Dilute

Adult: in 250–500 ml NS or D5%. Child: 5–10 ml/kg.

3

Start slow

1–2 ml/min for 10–15 min while watching for a reaction.

4

Speed up

If there is no reaction, increase the rate so the infusion finishes within one hour.

5

Observe

Monitor during the infusion and for at least one hour after. Chart vital signs and clinical progress.

Beyond the bite

Special situations

Venom ophthalmia

  • Spitting cobra (N. sumatrana)
  • Copious irrigation with saline, water, or any bland fluid
  • Fluorescein stain; urgent slit-lamp review
  • Topical anaesthetic (short term), cycloplegic, antibiotic if there is corneal erosion

No topical steroid (corneal melting) and no topical antivenom.

Neostigmine trial

  • For neurotoxic envenoming, best evidence in cobra (post-synaptic), not krait
  • Atropine 0.6 mg IV, then neostigmine 0.02 mg/kg IM (child 0.04 mg/kg)
  • Observe 30–60 min for less ptosis and better ventilatory capacity
  • Coral snakes: no AV, so this is the main option

Adequate antivenom comes first.

The bitten limb

  • Swelling ≠ compartment syndrome. Fasciotomy: NO on swelling alone; confirm with compartment pressure
  • Debride necrotic tissue later, once demarcated
  • Washout may help if venom was injected into a joint
  • Python bites: lacerations, antibiotics

Treat the venom with antivenom, not the knife.

Snakebite is a WHO neglected tropical disease (2017). The burden falls on rural, low-resource communities: farmers, plantation workers, children.

The answer is education, access to the right antivenom, and better reporting.

Summary

Six things to remember

01

Toxinology = living toxins

Venoms and natural toxins from animals, plants, microbes and fungi; a core ED skill.

02

Know the families

Elapids: neurotoxic ± local. Pit vipers: haematotoxic + local. Sea snakes: myotoxic. Pythons: mechanical injury.

03

First aid: do no harm

Calm, immobilise, remove jewellery, transport. No cutting, sucking, ligatures or herbs.

04

Serial assessment

Red zone, ABCDE, then watch closely for 48 h: PSP, RPP, neuro signs, bleeding, bloods, 20WBCT.

05

AV when indicated

One systemic or significant local criterion is enough. Right AV, right dose, adrenaline ready, repeat by the criteria.

06

Avoid the traps

No NSAIDs, IM injections, prophylactic steroids, TXA or vitamin K, or fasciotomy. Call RECS.
